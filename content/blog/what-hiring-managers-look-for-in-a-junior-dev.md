I've been on both sides of the interview table. I've applied for roles, and I've helped evaluate candidates. Here's what I've learned about what hiring managers actually look for in a junior developer.

## The Myth of the "Required" Tech Stack

Job postings list 15 technologies and call them "requirements." Here's the truth: most hiring managers know you won't know all of them.

What they actually care about:

1. **Can you learn fast?** — tech stacks change. Show that you can pick up new tools.
2. **Do you understand the fundamentals?** — data structures, algorithms, system design basics.
3. **Can you communicate?** — explain your thought process, ask good questions, accept feedback.
4. **Do you ship?** — projects, open source, anything that shows you finish what you start.

## What Stands Out on a Resume

### 1. Real Projects (Not Tutorials)

A todo list app from a YouTube tutorial tells me you can follow instructions. A student attendance system with actual GitHub stars and forks tells me you can build something people find useful.

Build things that solve real problems — even small ones.

### 2. Evidence of Growth

I love seeing old code next to new code. Show me your first GitHub commit, then show me something you built last week. The gap between them tells me more than any credential.

### 3. Writing

Blog posts, documentation, README files — writing shows you can think clearly and communicate effectively. Two of the most underrated skills in software engineering.

### 4. Open Source Contributions

Even small ones. A pull request that fixes a typo in documentation tells me you care enough to contribute. A bug fix tells me you can read someone else's code — one of the hardest skills to learn.

## What Matters in Interviews

### Communication Over Perfect Answers

I've never hired someone because they got every technical question right. I've hired people because they:

- Explained their thinking process clearly
- Asked clarifying questions when they didn't understand
- Admitted when they didn't know something
- Showed how they'd figure it out

### Curiosity Matters More Than Knowledge

The best junior engineers I've worked with share one trait: they're relentlessly curious. They ask "why" constantly. They dig into error messages. They want to understand how things work under the hood.

## Red Flags

- **Can't explain their own projects** — if you built it, you should be able to talk about it
- **Blames others** — hiring is about finding team players
- **Claims expertise in everything** — no one knows everything. Honest self-assessment is valuable
- **Doesn't ask questions** — interviews are two-way conversations

## What I Wish I Knew Earlier

### 1. Your Portfolio Is Your Resume
A well-documented GitHub profile with real projects is worth more than a formatted PDF. Link to your code, your live demos, your blog posts.

### 2. Network Before You Need To
Build relationships before you're looking for a job. Attend events, contribute to open source, engage on social media. When you're ready to apply, you'll have people who can vouch for you.

### 3. Apply Even If You Don't Meet All Requirements
Studies show men apply when they meet 60% of requirements; women apply when they meet 100%. Apply anyway. Let the hiring manager decide if you're qualified — don't decide for them.

### 4. Every Rejection Is Data
You didn't fail. You learned something about what the market needs. Use that information to get better.

## The Bottom Line

Hiring managers are looking for someone who:

- **Can learn** — adapting to new technologies quickly
- **Can communicate** — explaining ideas clearly
- **Can ship** — finishing what they start
- **Cares** — about quality, about users, about improvement

Everything else — the specific framework, the exact years of experience — is negotiable.
